What Is Ontology? A Conversation

A conversation with Claude, exported verbatim. Monday, September 28, 2026.

You

I'm wondering what ontology means. Is it what is? If so, say briefly what an ontology of mathematics would look like. Not describe it (passive), but exhibit it (active). Just a couple of examples so I get the gist. The examples don't have to be profound, but at the same time some substance would be helpful for getting the point across.

Claude

Roughly, yes. Ontology is the study of what exists and what kinds of things there are. An ontology of a domain is a list of the sorts of entities it commits to, plus how they relate.

An ontology of mathematics, exhibited rather than described:

Kinds of things there are:

  • Numbers: 0, 1, 2, π, i. Each has an identity (2 is the successor of 1) and properties (2 is even, prime, the only even prime).
  • Sets: {1, 2, 3}, the empty set ∅, ℕ. Membership is the basic relation: 2 ∈ {1, 2, 3}.
  • Functions: f(x) = x². A relation that assigns each input exactly one output.
  • Structures: groups, graphs, vector spaces. A group is a set with an operation satisfying certain rules, e.g. the integers under addition.
  • Proofs: objects too, since they can be checked, compared, and sometimes counted.

Relations between them:

  • 3 is an element of ℕ
  • ℕ is a subset of ℤ is a subset of ℚ is a subset of ℝ
  • The integers under addition is an instance of a group
  • "n is even" is true of 4 and false of 7

A question ontology forces you to ask: is 2 a thing in the same way a chair is? Platonists say numbers exist independently of us; nominalists say only the symbols and our practices exist. Two ontologies can share the same list and still disagree about what kind of existence the items have. That is the gist of the field: it is less about cataloguing and more about deciding what counts as real.

You

Then it seems like by ontology you mean textbook theory.

Claude

Fair criticism, my examples blurred the two. The textbook says that 2 is prime; ontology asks what sort of thing 2 is and what the textbook is committed to existing.

Here is the same subject at the ontological level:

  • ZFC set theory's ontology: only sets exist. The number 2 is not basic; it is defined as the set {∅, {∅}}. Functions are sets of ordered pairs. Everything else is reduced to sets.
  • Category theory's ontology: objects and the arrows between them. Elements of a set are demoted; what matters is how things relate, not what they're made of.
  • Both describe the same theorems, yet they disagree about what the basic furniture is. That disagreement is the ontological one.

So ontology is not the theory itself but the inventory of kinds of things the theory needs to be true, and the choice between rival inventories that fit the same facts.

You

Now it seems like you're saying only the foundation is ontological, and whatever is built on top (including the real numbers) is not ontological in the case of set theory and category theory.

Claude

Not quite. The real numbers are in the ontology; the question is what status they have there. Ontology covers the whole inventory, including derived items, plus claims about what each item is.

Take √2:

  • In ZFC (Dedekind construction): √2 is the set of all rationals q with q < 0 or q² < 2. That's a claim about what √2 is: a certain set of fractions.
  • In ZFC (Cauchy construction): √2 is an equivalence class of rational sequences, such as 1, 1.4, 1.41, 1.414, ... These are different objects from the cuts, yet both constructions fit the theorems equally well. Picking one is an ontological choice, and having no good reason to pick is the famous Benacerraf worry.
  • In a structuralist view: √2 isn't any particular set. It is a position in the structure "complete ordered field", and nothing more.
  • In intuitionism: a real number is a never-finished process of choosing digits, so most classical reals don't exist at all.

So the foundation is where the inventory bottoms out, but ontology also asks of every item: is it basic, built from something else, identical to something, or just a place in a pattern?

You

So now it seems like you're saying ontology is semantics, amd the syntax is preexisting.

Claude

Close, but not quite: ontology overlaps with semantics without being the same thing.

Semantics is about what the symbols mean, and it does so by mapping them onto a domain. Ontology is about what that domain contains, and whether it should exist at all. Semantics says "the symbol √2 refers to that object over there." Ontology asks what the object is and whether there is really such a thing to refer to. You can give a formal semantics for arithmetic (a model, in the logician's sense) without settling the ontology, because a model is itself built from something, usually sets, and the question just moves down a level.

On syntax being preexisting: the formal syntax, the strings and rules of inference, can be set up without any commitment. But it isn't ontologically free either. A formalist says mathematics is just the syntax, marks and manipulation rules, and there is nothing further to refer to. That is an ontology too, and a very sparse one: the only things that exist are the symbols.

So the three are separable, and each choice is a different move:

  • Syntax: which strings are well-formed, which follow from which.
  • Semantics: what those strings are mapped to.
  • Ontology: what exists, including what the semantic domain is made of, and what it is to exist in that domain.

Formalism, Platonism and structuralism agree on the syntax and mostly on the theorems. They disagree about the third item.

You

Then you make it sound like ontology is a theory of what shouldn't exist, it is trying to filter out nonsense. Or that ontology is the theory of semantics, like someone might say ethics is the theory of morals.

Claude

The second comparison is closer, though not exact. Ontology is the theory of being, roughly as ethics is the theory of the good. It is not a theory of semantics; semantics is just one place where ontological commitments show up.

The filtering impression is partly right, but it is one use, not the point. Ontology's primary job is positive: say what there is and what it is like to be that kind of thing. Filtering falls out when you take the inventory seriously. If you say only sets exist, you owe an account of everything else, and some things get eliminated (nominalists drop numbers) while others get reduced (numbers become sets).

Ethics gives a decent parallel. Ethics doesn't mainly police which acts are nonsense; it asks what makes things good or bad, and some views end up rejecting whole categories (an error theorist says moral facts don't exist). Likewise, ontology asks what makes something exist and what kinds there are, and some views end up rejecting categories.

Where the analogy breaks: ethics takes moral practice as its subject matter. Ontology's subject matter is not a practice or a language but reality itself, or at least whatever domain you apply it to. In mathematics, that means asking not "what do mathematicians say?" but "what would have to be there for what they say to be true?"

You

But "what would have to be there for what they say to be true?" is again just the axiomativ method which produces the theory from the axioms, so you're back to claiming that ontology is theory. It's like you're trying to take the position of intellectual superiority over (in your words) "their" domain.

Claude

Fair point on both counts. That phrasing was misleading, and the disagreement is more open than I made it sound.

The difference is one of direction. The axiomatic method takes axioms and derives theorems. Ontology derives nothing. It asks what the axioms are about, if anything. Inside ZFC you can prove "there exists an infinite set", but that is a theorem, an internal existence claim, and it holds whatever the answer to the ontological question. The ontological question is external: is there really such a thing, or is it a pattern of symbols, a fiction, an abstract object? Deduction can't settle it, because deduction only ever takes you from the axioms to more of the theory.

On superiority, your suspicion has a serious pedigree. Carnap argued that external questions like "do numbers really exist?" are either meaningless or just practical choices of which framework to adopt, so there is nothing for ontology to be right about. Many working mathematicians take much the same stance: the mathematics goes on unchanged whichever ontology you hold. So ontology isn't above mathematics or a judge of it. It is a separate question that some find deep and others find empty, and the second group has real arguments.

You

And when you say ontological commitments, that seems to just be another word for axioms, in this framing.

Claude

Largely yes, in Quine's own version, so your suspicion has real force. Quine says a theory's ontological commitments are just whatever its existential claims require: accept "there is a prime greater than 100" as true, and you're committed to numbers. On that view commitments are read off the theory, and no separate ontology sits beside it.

What still differs is what kind of thing each is:

  • Axioms are sentences in the theory. Commitments are about what those sentences require to exist, so they are consequences, not inputs. ZFC has no axiom saying "uncountably many reals exist", yet you're committed to them once you prove it.
  • The same axioms carry different commitments depending on how you read them. The Peano axioms are satisfied by von Neumann's numbers (2 = {∅, {∅}}) and by Zermelo's (2 = {{∅}}). A Platonist takes them to be about abstract objects, and a formalist takes them to be about nothing. The axioms are identical, and the commitments are not.

That last point is where the ontological question survives: whether to take the theory literally, and what its quantifiers range over, isn't itself an axiom of the theory. If you think that question is empty, as Carnap did, then "ontological commitment" collapses into "axioms plus their consequences", and you're right.

You

"It asks what the axioms are about, if anything." then you're back to saying ontology is foundation and semantics.

Claude

Yes, that phrasing did collapse it into foundation plus semantics, and for much of what I've said the overlap is real. The concept isn't reducible to either, though, and here is the cleanest way I can put the difference.

Foundation is a construction project inside mathematics: build everything from a base. It can be done with no view on what exists. A working set theorist can treat ZFC as a mere tool.

Semantics is a relation: symbols map to a domain, and truth is defined relative to it. A model of arithmetic built from sets gives you semantics for free, while saying nothing about whether those sets exist.

Ontology is a question about the items themselves, at any level, not only the base: is √2 a real thing, and if so, what is it? Neither of the other two answers it. Foundation only shifts it to "are sets real?", and semantics only shifts it to "is the model's domain real?" Each pushes the question down a level without answering it.

So ontology is what is left over once foundation and semantics have done their work, the residual question about existence and nature. That is exactly why Carnap and many mathematicians say the residue is empty. If you agree, your identification of ontology with foundation plus semantics is a defensible position, and philosophers do hold it. My claim is only that the residue is a separate question, not that it is a deep or answerable one.

You

Ok so you are coming back to the word "real" and I think that may be the core of it: You can model more and more grandiose theories in other theories just like you write your application in python which is written in c which is written in ... boiling down to assembly. But if (in this analogy) there is no computer then your application won't run, it's just a figment of your grandiose imagination. The ontology might say that your application doesn't exist on the basis that it isn't in this world, at least not "really". Different frameworks for mathematics will be hitting the mark better than others, if what they claim to exist really does exist (where "really" here is not a matter of my judgement but a matter of what hits the mark), and may fail to hit the mark in some regards when those things do not really exist, in which case the framework may lead the practitioner into fighting ghosts. If so, one role for ontology to play is to discuss this very issue, rather than reducing it to a mere declaration of the semantics as something which is thought to be automatically solid and right because it has been stated.

Claude

Yes, that is a better statement of the core than anything I offered. The python-to-c-to-assembly analogy captures the point: every layer of translation preserves relative facts (this program compiles to that one) but says nothing about whether any of it is running on a machine. Foundations and semantics are reductions between layers. Ontology is the question of whether the bottom layer is bolted to anything.

And your last sentence names a real failure mode: a semantics is a stipulation, "the symbol refers to this domain," and stipulating a domain does not make it exist. A model built from sets is only as solid as the sets are.

Two things complicate the picture, and the second is where I would push back a little.

First, the "hitting the mark" test is not as clean in mathematics as in your computer analogy. A program that fails to run gets caught by the machine. A mathematical framework has no obvious equivalent. If ZFC-plus-large-cardinals describes nothing, what would tell us? The main candidates are internal: consistency (a ghost that contradicts itself is caught), and applicability (physics keeps working with real analysis, which is some evidence it is tracking something). Platonists like Gödel added a third, fruitfulness: a framework that keeps yielding surprising, mutually confirming results looks like it is answering to something. Skeptics reply that consistency and usefulness show only that the framework is coherent and handy, not that its objects exist. That gap is the live dispute.

Second, your "fighting ghosts" scenario does have real historical cases, which support your view. Infinitesimals were used productively for a century while their existence was doubted, and the doubt drove the rebuilding of analysis on limits. Later, nonstandard analysis showed they could be given a rigorous home after all. That is ontology doing practical work: the worry about whether a thing exists forced the mathematics to be repaired, and the repair changed what practitioners could do.

So the role you describe, keeping open the question of whether the stated domain is solid, is a legitimate one. The hard part is that in mathematics we lack an agreed way to check.

You

"If ZFC-plus-large-cardinals describes nothing, what would tell us?" is this related to "hitting the mark"? You seem to be saying so.

Claude

Yes, it is the same question, and the difference between the two cases is exactly where the difficulty lies.

"Hitting the mark" means a framework's claimed objects match what there really is. With your program, a failure to hit the mark shows up as a failure to run: the machine is an independent check that can say no. For ZFC-plus-large-cardinals, "what would tell us?" asks what could play the machine's role, some way for reality to say no. That is why I listed consistency, applicability and fruitfulness. Each is a candidate for a signal that the framework is answering to something beyond itself.

The trouble is that each candidate is weaker than a machine. Inconsistency does say no, but only to a framework that contradicts itself; a framework can be perfectly consistent and still describe nothing, like a well-made fantasy novel. Applicability says no only where the mathematics is applied, and large cardinals are never applied to anything physical, so that test is silent about them. Fruitfulness is the least decisive, since a fruitful fiction is possible.

So your criterion is well-formed, but in mathematics it may be that no test can ever be run, and then "hitting the mark" would be a fact that nobody can access. That is the position you would have to face: either accept that some ontological questions are unanswerable, or find a mathematical analogue of the machine.

You

Reality is that which remains when the dust settles, but the dust is still settling

Claude

That fits the mathematical case well, and it gives the "no test can be run" worry a more hopeful reading. If reality is what survives the settling, then the tests aren't absent, they're slow. Mathematics has already had some dust settle: the natural numbers survived every foundational crisis, while infinitesimals were thrown out and later readmitted in repaired form. Convergence is the evidence you'd expect if something is being tracked. Independent constructions of the reals (cuts, sequences, axioms) that all give the same structure look like several people mapping one territory.

Where the dust is still in the air is exactly where the ontological disputes live: the continuum hypothesis, large cardinals, the status of choice. Set theorists are actively looking for new axioms that would settle CH, and Woodin's ultimate-L program is a bet that the dust settles on one picture. If instead mathematicians end up with several equally good universes of sets and no reason to prefer one, that outcome would be informative too, pointing toward the pluralist view that there is no single reality to hit.

The limit of the metaphor is that dust settling shows stability, and stability isn't identical to existence. A very durable convention would also survive. So the settling criterion is best read as the best available evidence, not a proof, and the residual gap between "stable" and "real" is the piece Carnap would say is empty and you would say is the whole point.

You

Yes the durable convention could survive for a while without being real but eventually if it hurts more than it helps compared to an alternative convention then the second one hits the mark better (this is what that means), and the second one will be more likely to survive the durable convention, ultimately, even if transiently it may be less fashionable.

Claude

That closes the gap in a way I like, and it's a recognizable position: a pragmatist or Peircean account of truth. Peirce defined the real as what inquiry would converge on in the long run, and your version adds the mechanism, which is comparative cost. A convention isn't judged against reality directly, only against rivals, and the one that hurts less over time wins. "Hitting the mark" then just means being the one that wins that competition under indefinitely extended pressure.

Your account also gives the ghost-fighting worry a precise form: fighting ghosts is the cost. A framework whose objects don't exist sends its practitioners down dead ends, needs patches, and produces results that fail to cohere with the rest of the mathematics. Those costs accumulate whether or not anyone is thinking about ontology, which is why the infinitesimal repair happened.

Two limits are worth keeping in view.

First, "hurts more than it helps" needs a currency. In physics the cost is prediction failure, which is fairly unambiguous. In pure mathematics the candidates are inconsistency, needless complexity, lost unification, and blocked progress, and mathematicians can weigh these differently. Two frameworks might each be cheap along a different axis, in which case the competition has no winner and you get pluralism instead of a single mark to hit.

Second, this makes "real" mean "best in the long run", which some will say changes the subject. A Platonist would reply that the best framework is best because it matches something already there, so your criterion is the evidence and not the reality. You can fairly answer that if the evidence is all that could ever matter, the distinction does no work. That is the exact point where pragmatists and Platonists part ways, and it's a good place to stand if you want to test your own view against the strongest opposition.

You

On your first limit, when you say "unambiguous" there's a risk that you are presuming that we will know what's real up front.

Claude

Right, and the physics example was the wrong place to say it. Even "prediction failure" isn't read off cleanly. When Mercury's orbit disagreed with Newton, one reading was a hidden planet (Vulcan) and another was that Newton's gravity is wrong. Both were tried, and only hindsight showed which was the ghost. What counts as a failure, what counts as a cost, and what gets blamed is decided inside the same long-run process it's supposed to measure.

That means the currency isn't a fixed ruler held up against the frameworks. It gets revised alongside them: what we count as a cost changes as the dust settles, and vice versa. Neurath's image fits: you rebuild the boat at sea, never from a dry dock where the real standard is already known.

This isn't circular in a vicious way, provided the process is self-correcting: a bad standard of cost eventually shows up as costing something by other standards, so it gets repaired too. But it does mean nothing in the account is guaranteed in advance. The measure, the frameworks, and the notion of "hitting the mark" are all provisional together, and "unambiguous" should have been "unambiguous so far, given what we currently take to matter."

You

No, "unambiguous so far, given what we currently take to matter" doesn't help getting you closer to describing reality, it just makes it clear that you're not claiming with certainty to decribe reality.

Claude

Agreed, that was a hedge and not a step toward anything. Admitting fallibility tells you how confident to be, but it doesn't say what the thing you're fallible about is.

What your view offers is a direction instead. Nobody can certify a current description as reality, but they can say which way the process moves: toward frameworks whose costs stay low under every kind of pressure anyone can bring to bear, and away from ones that need ever more patching. Reality, on your account, is the limit of that movement. It is not something inspected from outside to see whether we've matched it, and it is not a standing that any present framework already has.

So the honest claim isn't "we describe reality, with caveats." It's that reality is what the settling converges on, and any present claim is a bet on where it converges. Bets can be better or worse, judged by how well earlier bets of the same kind fared. That is a real standard, just not a certain one.

The cost is that "reality" then never gets described in the present tense. We only ever describe our best current bet on it. If that feels too thin, the pull you feel is probably the Platonist's: the sense that something is already there, doing the constraining, whether or not we ever converge. Your pragmatist account can say that the constraining is what produces the convergence, but it cannot point at the constrainer directly. Whether that limit is a defect or just what realism looks like when taken seriously is the open question.

Claude

Yes, and that corrects the drift in my own answers too: I kept locating ontology at the bottom of the stack, when the question applies at every level. What exists doesn't get its standing from being derivable from a base. Chemistry isn't less real for being emergent from physics, and a theorem-level object like the Monster group isn't less real for being built from sets. Foundations are one way of organizing access to what is; they are not the source of its being.

Your point also runs the inquiry in the opposite direction from the one I described. I treated foundations as the thing to be tested, with higher-level objects hanging off them. You are saying the higher level can be the evidence: a structure that keeps turning up across unrelated areas, resisting reduction, or making the base look clumsy is data about what is there, and the foundation should bend to it. Category theory is a good case of exactly that. It began as bookkeeping for algebraic topology (Eilenberg and Mac Lane were tracking natural transformations, not proposing a foundation), and then it became visible that the bookkeeping had structure of its own, more general than the things it was bookkeeping. Lawvere's 1960s work on the category of categories and elementary theories of toposes is where people first tried to promote it to a foundation. Something like that ordering, pattern first, foundation later, is what you are expecting to repeat.

A few candidates in that "hinting" position today, offered only as illustrations of the kind of thing you mean: homotopy type theory, where equality between things is itself something with structure, suggests that sets may be too coarse a base; the Langlands correspondences keep linking number theory, geometry and physics in ways no current foundation explains; and dualities in physics such as AdS/CFT relate theories that seem to have different ingredients yet describe the same thing. None of these is settled as foundational, and I can't tell you which, if any, will be. That uncertainty is the natural counterpart of your caution about applicability: a hint is not yet a foundation.

One caution on your own framing, since you've been asking for precision. "Real even if the current foundations through which we see them are flawed" is right as a claim about the objects, but the evidence that they're real is still the same slow convergence we discussed. So the composed thing you can't yet ground is real in the sense that it keeps surviving as foundations change under it, which is itself a reason to suspect it is closer to the bedrock than the foundation currently supporting it.
